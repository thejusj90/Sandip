BREATH & SCIENCE

The Neuroscience of Breath and Choice

What happens in the brain and body when we consciously change our breathing? Explore the emerging science connecting breath, nervous-system regulation and conscious response.

Breathing happens roughly all day without requiring us to think about it.

Yet unlike many automatic bodily processes, breathing can also be deliberately changed.

You can slow it.

Notice it.

Lengthen an exhalation.

Or simply pay attention to the next breath.

This unusual combination (automatic yet voluntarily accessible) is one reason breathing has attracted growing scientific interest.

The short answer

Breathing interacts with the nervous system, cardiovascular system and brain. Changing the rhythm of breathing can influence physiological arousal, while noticing the breath can increase awareness of what is happening inside the body. Together, these processes may help create conditions in which a person can respond more deliberately rather than react automatically.

That does not mean one breath rewires your brain.

The reality is more interesting, and more nuanced.

Breathing is connected to the autonomic nervous system

Our autonomic nervous system helps regulate processes such as heart rate, digestion and physiological responses to changing circumstances.

Breathing interacts closely with this system.

In particular, research into slower breathing has observed changes in measures including heart-rate variability and parasympathetic activity.

This matters because our physical state influences how we experience situations.

Consider how differently the same conversation feels when you are:

rested versus exhausted,

calm versus rushed,

comfortable versus physically tense.

The mind does not operate separately from the body.

Our internal physiological state becomes part of how we experience the world.

Your brain is constantly receiving information from your body

The perception of signals originating inside the body is often discussed through the concept of interoception.

Heartbeat.

Breathing.

Muscular tension.

Temperature.

Sensations in the stomach.

These signals contribute to the brain's representation of our internal state and are increasingly studied in relation to emotion and self-regulation.

Paying attention to breathing therefore does something very simple but potentially important:

it directs attention inward.

Instead of being completely absorbed by the external event:

What did that person say?

What should I reply?

What will happen next?

Attention briefly includes:

What is happening in me right now?

That change in perspective can matter.

Breathing and the brain influence each other

Research increasingly suggests that respiration is not simply a mechanical process supplying the body with oxygen and removing carbon dioxide.

Respiratory rhythms interact with neural activity, and scientists are studying how those interactions may relate to cognition, attention and emotional processing.

The field is still developing, so claims such as "this breathing technique activates this exact brain region and makes you calm" should be treated cautiously.

Human nervous systems are more complicated than an infographic.

A more defensible interpretation is:

breathing is one part of a larger brain-body regulatory system.

Changing one part of that system can influence the state of the whole.

From physiology to choice

Suppose somebody says something that makes you angry.

The sequence might feel instantaneous:

event → emotion → reaction

But there is considerable processing occurring between those points.

Your brain interprets the situation.

Your body changes.

Attention shifts.

Memories and expectations become involved.

You prepare to act.

Becoming aware of your breathing does not erase this process.

Instead, it can give you something to notice while the process is happening.

And that is where choice becomes interesting.

You may still decide to disagree.

You may still speak firmly.

You may still leave the room.

The difference is that the behaviour becomes less automatic.

The breath is not the destination

This distinction matters.

The purpose of breath awareness is not to spend your life monitoring every inhalation.

Breath is an entry point.

A way of learning to recognise:

"I am becoming defensive."

"I am rushing."

"My body is tense."

"I've stopped listening."

"I am about to respond from habit."

The deeper practice is awareness.

Breath simply gives us a reliable place to begin.

Try this before your next difficult conversation

Do not try to breathe perfectly.

Instead:

Take one comfortable breath.

Notice the inhalation.

Notice the exhalation.

Notice the state of your body.

Then ask yourself:

What response would I like to choose here?

That question is where breathing moves beyond relaxation.

It becomes a practice of conscious response.

Frequently asked questions

How does breathing affect the nervous system?

Breathing interacts with autonomic cardiovascular regulation. Studies of slow breathing have reported changes in parasympathetic activity and heart-rate variability, although individual responses and protocols vary.

Does slow breathing reduce stress?

Research suggests slow-breathing practices may reduce self-reported stress or anxiety for some people. A 2023 meta-analysis found generally promising effects from breathwork while also calling for more rigorous research.

Can breathing change the brain?

Respiration interacts with neural activity, and researchers are studying connections between breathing rhythms, cognition and emotion. It would be an oversimplification, however, to claim that a particular breath instantly "rewires" the brain.

Why focus on breathing rather than thoughts?

Thoughts can be difficult to observe while we are caught inside them. Breathing provides a continuously available physical sensation that can act as an anchor for attention.

Experience the practice, not just the idea.
