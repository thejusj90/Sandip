# How to Design a Community Impact Pilot

**Article category:** Impact Advisory  
**Publication date:** Add the actual date when published  
**Suggested author:** Confirm byline before publication

A resort can advertise a local partnership and still give local producers little predictable income. A community programme can fill its first session and still be out of reach for the people it was designed to serve. In both cases the activity happened. The question is whether anything important changed for the intended group.

A community impact pilot tests one plausible route from an organisation’s action to a shared outcome. Its job is to help you decide what to do next with the evidence you can realistically collect.

## Agree the outcome with the people affected

Begin by asking what people want to change and what already helps or gets in the way. Listen beyond the partners who are easiest to reach. A producer who never joined a resort’s network may explain barriers that existing suppliers work around. Consider who can participate, how participation will be supported and how disagreements will be recorded.

Use a single outcome statement. For example, “Nearby small producers can secure repeat paid orders on terms they can fulfil” is more useful than “strengthen community engagement.” The former identifies a group and a change you can look for.

## Write down why the pilot might work

A short theory of change connects the proposed action to the desired outcome and names the assumptions between them. In the illustrative resort example, a resort could offer advance purchase commitments, an agreed payment timetable and batch sizes producers can meet. The expected result is more predictable orders and payment. That logic depends on guest demand, production capacity, workable collection logistics and the operator honouring terms.

Name the alternative, too. An occasional exhibition might offer visibility but provide fewer repeat orders. Comparing routes before launch helps the team choose what is worth testing and what evidence would change its mind.

## Choose measures that tell you more than attendance

Track outputs such as vendors onboarded, as well as early outcomes such as repeat paid orders or time between delivery and payment. Record the starting point, who owns each measure and how often it will be reviewed. Where feasible and appropriate, look at differences between groups. An average can obscure whether a pilot helped established suppliers while excluding newer ones.

Ask about unplanned costs and harms. A producer could receive more orders but take on unpaid travel or waste from last-minute cancellations. Keep data collection proportionate and explain what you will use the information for.

## Agree the decision rules before you start

Schedule a review and agree what would support continuing, adapting, stopping or scaling. A team might continue if orders are repeated and payments arrive as agreed. It might adapt if transport makes participation impractical. It should be willing to stop if the burden falls on the very people the pilot was intended to benefit.

Improvement during a short pilot does not establish that the pilot caused it. Seasonality, demand, other programmes and differences between participants may all matter. State what you observed, what the pilot may have contributed and what remains uncertain. If the decision calls for independence, commission an evaluator who did not design or run the work.

## What to bring to the first meeting

Bring one decision or outcome, the people you think may be affected, the deadline and what you already know. The first piece of work may be to listen and compare options. Where the direction is clearer, you can scope a pilot and establish the baseline.
