Why "Doing More" Doesn't Work (and What Does)

Constantly trying harder can leave us exhausted without creating meaningful change. Explore why awareness, space and small intentional responses can work differently.

When something in life is not working, our instinct is often simple:

Try harder.

Add another routine. Read another book. Download another app. Set another goal. Push ourselves a little more.

Sometimes that helps.

But there are also moments when doing more becomes part of the problem.

We become increasingly busy trying to improve ourselves while repeating many of the same reactions, habits and patterns.

The short answer

Lasting change does not always begin with more effort. It often begins by creating enough awareness to notice what is happening before automatically responding to it.

That small space between stimulus and response can give us an opportunity to make a different choice.

And one surprisingly accessible place to practise noticing that space is something we are already doing all day:

breathing.

Why effort alone can fail

Imagine receiving an email that immediately irritates you.

Before you consciously decide what to do, several things may already be happening.

Your body becomes tense. Your attention narrows. Thoughts accelerate. You begin constructing the reply in your head.

Then you respond.

Later, you may wonder why you reacted so quickly.

The difficulty was not necessarily a lack of knowledge. You probably already knew that responding calmly would have been better.

The challenge was that the reaction happened faster than the deliberate choice.

Many of our behaviours work this way.

Behavioural research suggests that repeated behaviours can gradually become associated with particular contexts and cues. As those associations strengthen, the behaviour can require less conscious deliberation.

Simply telling ourselves to "do better next time" may therefore be insufficient.

We first have to notice the pattern.

The difference between reaction and response

A reaction is often fast.

A response includes a little more space.

That space does not need to last five minutes.

Sometimes it is one breath.

One moment of noticing:

  • My shoulders have tightened.
  • I am speaking faster.
  • I want to interrupt.
  • I am already deciding what the other person means.

The objective is not to eliminate emotion.

It is to recognise what is happening early enough that another response becomes possible.

Why breath can become a useful anchor

Breathing is unusual because it operates automatically while also being something we can consciously influence.

Scientific research into slow breathing has found associations with changes in autonomic activity, heart-rate variability and psychological states related to stress and emotional regulation. Researchers are still working to understand the exact mechanisms and the effects vary across techniques and individuals.

But breath does not have to become another complicated self-improvement project.

Its simplest function may be this:

it gives attention somewhere to return.

When you notice your breathing, you temporarily interrupt the stream of thoughts, predictions and automatic responses.

That interruption can create a moment of awareness.

And awareness creates the possibility of choice.

Try less, but notice more

Instead of asking:

"What else should I do?"

occasionally ask:

"What is already happening?"

Notice what happens when:

  • someone challenges your opinion;
  • you are running late;
  • a meeting becomes uncomfortable;
  • you feel pressure to give an immediate answer;
  • you receive unexpected criticism;
  • you are about to say yes when you would rather say no.

You do not have to correct the reaction immediately.

Start by noticing it.

That itself is a practice.

A simple practice: one conscious breath

The next time you notice yourself reacting quickly:

  1. Stop for a moment.
  2. Notice where you feel the reaction in your body.
  3. Allow one comfortable breath in.
  4. Let the breath out without forcing it.
  5. Then decide what you want to do next.

It may sound almost too simple.

That is precisely the point.

Transformation does not always require adding something dramatic to our lives.

Sometimes it begins with becoming more aware of the life already happening.

What does "doing less" actually mean?

Doing less does not mean becoming passive, unambitious or unproductive.

It means recognising that effort and awareness are different things.

You can work extremely hard while remaining trapped in automatic patterns.

Or you can develop the ability to notice those patterns and respond more deliberately.

The goal is not less action.

It is more conscious action.

Frequently asked questions

Why doesn't trying harder always create change?

Because many behaviours become strongly associated with situations, emotions and environmental cues. Motivation helps, but awareness, repetition and context also influence behavioural change.

Can breathing help with self-awareness?

Paying attention to breathing is commonly used as a way of bringing attention back to present bodily experience. Research into breath-focused and interoceptive practices suggests links between bodily awareness and emotional processing, although the science continues to evolve.

How long should I practise?

You do not necessarily need a long session to begin developing awareness. Start by deliberately noticing a single breath at moments when you would ordinarily react automatically.

What is the goal of conscious breathing?

In this context, the goal is not perfect breathing. It is creating a moment in which you can notice your state before deciding how to respond.

Experience the practice, not just the idea.
